1. THE PROJECT IN ONE PARAGRAPH
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Two food-delivery apps (Platform A and Platform B, think Swiggy and
Zomato) compete in the same city during the dinner rush. They share the
same customers and the same pool of delivery riders (riders can work for
either app). If A pays riders more, riders leave B, so B's deliveries get
slower. Each app's result therefore depends on what the OTHER app does.
That is exactly what game theory studies. We build one model of this
city, then let the apps compete in different ways (choose riders, choose
pay, choose fees, move first, play every day, have secret information)
and compare the results.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
2. THE STORY: THE MARKET WE MODEL
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything happens in ONE peak hour in ONE city zone. Money is in rupees.

  CUSTOMERS
    - Up to Λ (lambda) orders per hour could be placed:
        Low = 600, Medium = 1000, Peak = 1500, Extreme = 2000.
    - Each customer chooses: order from A, order from B, or don't order.
    - They prefer a LOW delivery fee and a SHORT delivery time.

  RIDERS
    - There are R = 500 riders in the city.
    - Each rider chooses: work for A, work for B, or stay offline.
    - They prefer the app that pays MORE per order. If neither pays
      more than about ₹25 (their "reservation pay" w0), many stay offline.

  DELIVERY TIME (DELAY)
    - Each rider can do about k = 2.2 deliveries per hour.
    - "Utilisation" ρ (rho) = orders ÷ capacity. ρ = 0.5 means the fleet
      is half busy; ρ close to 1 means everyone is busy.
    - Delay = 28 minutes + an extra amount that shoots up as ρ gets
      close to 1. Formula: D = 28 + 6·ρ/(1−ρ).
      ρ = 0.5 -> 34 min,  ρ = 0.75 -> 46 min,  ρ = 0.9 -> 82 min.

  THE LOOP
    - More orders -> busier riders -> longer delay -> fewer customers
      order -> less busy ... The model finds the point where this loop
      settles down (the "fixed point").

  PROFIT OF A PLATFORM (per hour)
    profit = orders delivered × (delivery fee + restaurant commission − rider pay)
           = orders × (p + 45 − w)
    with p = ₹35 fee (unless the model lets apps choose it), m = ₹45
    commission from the restaurant, and w = rider pay per order.

  WELFARE (is the outcome good for society?)
    - Consumer surplus  = how happy customers are (good choices, short waits).
    - Platform profit   = what the two apps earn.
    - Rider surplus     = how much better off riders are than offline.
    - Total welfare     = all three added up.
    - Efficiency        = our welfare ÷ the best welfare an ideal planner
                          could reach. 100% = perfect, 80% = 20% wasted.

3. GAME THEORY WORDS YOU NEED (GLOSSARY)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Player          A decision-maker. Here: Platform A and Platform B.
Strategy        What a player chooses. Here: number of riders (N),
                rider pay (w) or delivery fee (p).
Payoff          What a player gets. Here: profit in ₹ per hour.
Best response   Your best choice GIVEN what the rival does.
                "If B pays ₹40, my best pay is ₹47."
Best-response   A line showing your best choice for every possible rival
curve           choice. Two curves, one per platform.
Nash            A pair of choices where each is a best response to the
equilibrium     other, so nobody wants to change alone. On a chart, it is
(NE)            where the two best-response curves CROSS.
Unilateral      Changing your own choice while the rival stays put.
deviation       The app checks that no deviation increases profit; that
                is how we know a result really is a Nash equilibrium.
Cournot         Competing by choosing QUANTITIES (here: how many riders).
Bertrand        Competing by choosing PRICES (here: rider pay or fees).
Bertrand        If two firms sell identical things and compete on price,
paradox         price falls until profit is zero, even with only two firms.
Stackelberg     One player moves first (leader), the other sees that and
                responds (follower).
Backward        Solving a sequential game from the END: first work out how
induction       the follower reacts to anything, then pick the leader's
                best move knowing that.
Subgame-perfect The equilibrium found by backward induction (no player makes
equilibrium     a non-credible threat at any stage).
(SPE)
First-mover     Extra profit the leader gets compared with deciding at the
advantage (FMA) same time as the rival.
Strategic       My best choice goes DOWN when the rival's goes up (hiring
substitutes     riders: if B hires many, A hires fewer).
Strategic       My best choice goes UP when the rival's goes up (rider pay:
complements     if B pays more, A must pay more).
Payoff matrix   A table: rows = A's options, columns = B's options, each
                cell = (A's profit, B's profit).
Dominated       An option that is ALWAYS worse than another option, no
strategy        matter what the rival does. A sensible player never uses it.
IESDS           "Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies":
                cross out dominated options, then check again, repeat.
Mixed strategy  Choosing randomly with fixed probabilities (e.g. 50% heads,
                50% tails in Matching Pennies).
Maxmin          The "safest" choice: the one whose WORST possible result is
                as good as possible.
Prisoner's      Both players have a reason to cheat, so both cheat, and both
Dilemma (PD)    end up worse off than if both had cooperated.
                Rule: T > R > P > S
                  T = Temptation (I cheat, you don't)
                  R = Reward     (we both cooperate)
                  P = Punishment (we both cheat)
                  S = Sucker     (I cooperate, you cheat)
Collusion       The apps secretly agree to keep rider pay low (cooperate).
Repeated game   The same game played every day.
Discount        How much tomorrow matters compared with today (0 to 1).
factor δ        δ = 0.9: tomorrow's profit is worth 90% of today's.
Critical δ*     The minimum patience needed for cooperation to survive.
                δ* = (T − R) / (T − P). If δ ≥ δ*, nobody cheats.
Grim trigger    "I cooperate until you cheat once; then I punish forever."
Tit-for-Tat     "I start nice, then copy whatever you did yesterday."
(TFT)
Generous TFT    Like TFT, but sometimes forgives a defection.
(GTFT)
Noise           A chance that a fair move is SEEN as cheating by mistake.
Bayesian game   A game where players have private information.
Type            The private information. Here: is MY demand High or Low today.
Prior           The probability a player's type is High (e.g. 50%).
Bayesian Nash   Each type's choice is the best response to the rival's
equilibrium     expected (average) behaviour.
Social planner  An imaginary neutral controller that maximises total welfare.
Price of        Best welfare ÷ our welfare. 1.10 = competition wastes ~10%.
anarchy (PoA)
Logit choice    A standard way to model choices: the better an option is,
                the more people pick it, but not 100% (people differ).
Fixed point     A value that stays the same when you plug it back in. Our
                demand and delay must agree with each other at the fixed point.
Monte Carlo     Running a random simulation many times (20 seeds) and averaging.

5. THE 9 MODELS, ONE BY ONE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
All models use the SAME city, riders and customers. Only "what the apps
choose" and "who moves when" changes.

(1) COURNOT (capacity)
    Apps choose HOW MANY RIDERS to hire, at the same time. More riders
    cost more because you must pay more per order to attract them.
    Result: about 184-224 riders each, pay ₹27-35 per order.

(2) BERTRAND ON RIDER PAY (incentives)
    Apps choose RIDER PAY per order, at the same time. Riders go to the
    higher payer, so each app is tempted to outbid the other: the
    "incentive war". Result: pay climbs to ₹37-63 per order.
    Toggle "riders see both apps as identical" -> BERTRAND PARADOX: pay
    rises to ~₹79-80 (all the money they earn per order) and profit
    falls to nearly zero.

(3) BERTRAND ON FEES
    The number of riders is fixed. Apps choose the DELIVERY FEE. A lower
    fee brings orders but also makes the fleet busier and slower.

(4) STACKELBERG - capacity
    A hires first, B sees it and hires. A grabs more riders (leader
    advantage). B hires fewer and does worse.

(5) STACKELBERG - rider pay
    A sets pay first, B responds. A sets a moderate pay to calm the war.
    B then pays a bit more and actually gains MORE than A
    (second-mover advantage).

(6) TWO-STAGE: capacity, then fees
    Step 1: both hire riders. Step 2: knowing fleet sizes, both set fees.
    Solved backwards. Result: they hire almost exactly as many riders as
    in Cournot (within 0.7%). This is the famous Kreps-Scheinkman result.

(7) COLLUSION (benchmark)
    The apps act as one and keep rider pay low to maximise joint profit.
    Highest profit, but bad for riders (low pay) and customers (slower
    deliveries), and not stable on its own (each app is tempted to cheat).

(8) SOCIAL PLANNER (benchmark)
    A neutral planner picks pay and fees for both apps to maximise total
    welfare. Used as the "100%" for the efficiency score. At peaks it
    raises fees to reduce congestion ("congestion pricing").

(9a) STRATEGIC-FORM GAME (Low / Medium / High pay table)
    Low = the collusion pay, High = the best way to cheat on it.
    Result: High is always the better choice whatever the rival does, so
    both pick High, yet both would earn more at Low -> Prisoner's Dilemma.

(9b) REPEATED GAME
    The table above is played every day. With grim trigger, the truce
    survives if δ ≥ δ* (0.51 at Low demand, 0.71 at Extreme demand).

(9c) BAYESIAN (incomplete information)
    Each app knows if its own demand is High or Low, not the rival's.
    High-demand apps hire more riders. Not knowing the rival makes both
    types hire closer to the middle.

 2. MODEL PLAYGROUND

    - Green badge: the app checked nobody can gain by switching alone.
    - 4 cards: delivery time, orders delivered, profit, paid to riders.

 5. LEADER-FOLLOWER
    - Game tree: A's possible moves -> B's reply -> payoffs.

    - Leader's profit curve: the peak is A's best first move.
    - First-mover advantage bars for each demand level.

 6. REPEATED GAME LAB
    - δ slider with the red δ* marker.
    - "Cooperate forever vs deviate once" chart: where the lines cross

    - Tournament: 5 strategies play each other; bars = average profit.
    - Cumulative profit chart for any pair of strategies.
    - Noise chart: how cooperation falls when moves are mis-seen.

 7. INCOMPLETE INFORMATION
    - Riders hired by each app for High and Low demand.

 8. CITY SIMULATION
    - Animated map: dots = riders (blue A, orange B; filled busy,

---------------------------------------------------------------------
7. THE MAIN FINDINGS (WHAT WE LEARNED)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. Incentive wars mostly move money from platforms to riders.
    At Extreme demand, the war costs the apps about ₹28,000/hour more
    than colluding, but delivery is only 2.8 minutes faster. About 84%
    of the extra money ends up with riders. Reason: once all 500
    riders are working, extra pay cannot create new riders; it just
    pulls riders from one app to the other.

 2. Moving first helps when choosing RIDER NUMBERS (leader grabs
    riders), but when choosing RIDER PAY the follower gains even more.

 3. The low-pay truce is hardest to keep at peak times: the needed
    patience δ* rises from 0.51 (Low) to 0.71 (Extreme), because the
    temptation to steal riders is biggest when demand is highest.

 4. Mistakes break trust: with 5% chance of mis-seeing a fair move,
    grim trigger cooperates only ~19% of days; generous TFT ~80%.

 5. Committing to fleet size before setting fees gives almost the same
    fleet as Cournot (within 0.7%): Kreps-Scheinkman.

 6. Collusion is (almost) always the WORST for society; competition is
    closer to the best outcome. At peak, letting fees rise (congestion
    pricing) helps the most.

 7. The simple delay formula is checked by the city simulation: it is
    close at peak (within ~7%) and a bit pessimistic at moderate load
    (overall error ~17%).

8. HOW THE COMPUTER FINDS THE ANSWERS (SIMPLY)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
 - Fixed point: start with a guess for delay, compute orders, compute
   the new delay, adjust, repeat until it stops changing (Newton method).
 - Best response: try many values of the platform's choice (a grid of
   61 values), pick the best, then zoom in for precision.
 - Nash equilibrium: A best-responds to B, then B to A, repeat until
   nothing changes ("best-response iteration").
 - Check: for each equilibrium, try 401 other choices for each app
   with the rival fixed. If none earns more, the equilibrium is
   confirmed (green badge).
 - Stackelberg / two-stage: for each leader move, solve the follower's
   reply; then pick the leader's best move (backward induction).
 - Table games: cross out dominated options; solve small equations to
   find mixed equilibria.
 - Repeated game: simulate 60 days for every pair of strategies.
 - City simulation: every rider and order is simulated minute by
   minute; repeated with 20 random seeds and averaged.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
9. TWO CHANGES WE MADE TO THE ORIGINAL BRIEF, AND WHY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
 (a) Customer "attractiveness" a = 4.0 instead of 1.0.
     With a = 1.0, a customer's value of ordering was −3 versus 0 for
     not ordering, so only ~8% of customers ever ordered. Riders were
     never busy, delays never rose, and there was no incentive war,
     so nothing interesting could be studied. a = 4.0 changes no
     formula, only the level, and creates the busy-peak situation the
     project is about. It is still a slider.

 (b) Delay cost ₹3/min counted once, only for EXTRA delay above 28 min.
     Customers already "feel" ₹1.6/min of delay when choosing, so we
     add only the remaining ₹1.4/min. Counting it twice made customer
     happiness negative even on quiet days.

12. LIKELY VIVA QUESTIONS WITH SHORT ANSWERS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Q: Why is game theory needed here?
A: Each app's profit depends on the other app's choice (shared riders and
   customers), and there is no central controller. That is a strategic
   game, not a simple optimisation.

Q: How do you know your answer is really a Nash equilibrium?
A: For every result we try 401 alternative choices for each app with the
   rival fixed; none earns more. We also test textbook cases (Cournot
   q* = (a−c)/3b, Stackelberg (a−c)/2b, Matching Pennies 50/50,
   δ* = 0.5 for the classic Prisoner's Dilemma).

Q: Cournot vs Bertrand: what is the difference here?
A: Cournot = choose number of riders; Bertrand = choose rider pay. Paying
   per rider-number leads to calmer pay (₹27-35); competing on pay leads
   to an incentive war (₹37-63).

Q: What is the Bertrand paradox in your project?
A: If riders see both apps as identical, each app outbids the other until
   rider pay equals what it earns per order (₹80), so profit ≈ 0.

Q: Why does the follower win in the pay version of Stackelberg?
A: Pay levels are strategic complements: when A commits to a moderate pay,
   B can pay slightly more, take riders, and earn more than A.

Q: Why is the incentive game a Prisoner's Dilemma?
A: Whatever the rival does, paying High earns more than paying Low (T > R
   and P > S), so both pay High; but both paying Low (R) beats both
   paying High (P).

Q: What is δ* and what did you find?
A: The minimum patience for cooperation to survive under grim trigger,
   δ* = (T−R)/(T−P). It rises from 0.51 to 0.71 as demand rises, so
   truces are hardest at peak times.

Q: What does the Bayesian model show?
A: When an app's demand is private, it hires based on the rival's
   average behaviour, so High and Low types hire closer to each other
   than they would with full information.

Q: What is efficiency / price of anarchy?
A: Efficiency = our total welfare ÷ the planner's best welfare. Price of
   anarchy is the inverse. They measure how much competition wastes.

Q: Why the simulation?
A: To check the simple delay formula with a detailed, rider-by-rider
   model. It matches within ~7% at peak and is ~17% off on average.

Q: Biggest limitation?
A: One zone, one hour, illustrative (not real Swiggy/Zomato) numbers,
   and a simple delay formula. The direction of the results matters
   more than the exact rupee values.
