## 1. Key findings

1. **Incentive wars mostly transfer surplus to riders.** Moving from the incentive cartel to one-shot incentive (Bertrand) competition raises incentive spend by INR 28,407/h in the Extreme scenario but cuts average delay by only 2.8 min (about INR 10,273 per minute saved). Roughly 84% of the extra spend reappears as rider surplus: the war is mainly a transfer from platforms to riders, because once the shared pool is exhausted extra incentives only move riders between platforms.

2. **Which market structure comes closest to the planner?** Low: best Bertrand (incentives) (91.7% of first-best), worst Collusion (joint profit) (84.1%). Medium: best Bertrand (incentives) (99.4% of first-best), worst Bertrand (fees) (83.0%). Peak: best Bertrand (incentives) (97.3% of first-best), worst Bertrand (fees) (80.1%). Extreme: best Two-stage (capacity, then fees) (95.3% of first-best), worst Collusion (joint profit) (76.3%). Incentive competition scores well on efficiency even though it hurts platforms: incentives are transfers, and the war pulls the whole rider pool online, which is what the planner wants too. Platform losses reappear as rider surplus. The cartel is worst because it withholds riders to save on incentives.

3. **When does moving first help?** Committing to capacity first always helps the leader: the first-mover advantage grows from INR 73/h (Low) to INR 2,908/h (Extreme) because capacities are strategic substitutes. Committing to an incentive also raises the leader's profit (INR 2,149/h in Extreme) by softening the war, but incentives are strategic complements, so the follower gains even more: a second-mover advantage.

4. **How patient must platforms be to avoid an incentive war?** With grim-trigger strategies the cooperative (low-incentive) outcome is sustainable when the daily discount factor is at least 0.51 in the Low scenario, rising to 0.71 in the Extreme scenario. Peaks raise the one-shot temptation to poach riders, so tacit collusion is hardest exactly when demand is highest. Reverting to the full one-shot equilibrium as punishment lowers the threshold to 0.32.

5. **Capacity then fees reproduces Cournot capacities.** When platforms commit to rider capacity before setting fees, equilibrium capacities differ from the Cournot capacities by at most 0.7% across scenarios, the Kreps-Scheinkman intuition. Fees then rise with demand (INR 30 to 51) and act as congestion prices, which makes this the most efficient competitive structure at Extreme demand.

6. **Peaks widen the gap to the first-best.** Under incentive competition average delay rises from 31.4 min (Low) to 42.5 min (Extreme). The planner holds it at 36.6 min by raising fees at peak (congestion pricing) rather than paying more incentives, since the rider pool is already fully engaged.
